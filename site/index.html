Attention, in order

Every attention mechanism since 2014 is a response to a cost created by the one before it. This page starts with the one mechanism everything else modifies, then walks the field in the order ideas actually appeared, dated from the original paper or release. For each one: what existed, what hurt, what changed, what it buys, what it gives up, and when you would pick it.

Start here · 12 Jun 2017

Scaled dot-product attention, one step at a time

Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax( QKᵀ / √dk + M ) · V

Five tokens. Each token has a query vector (what it is looking for), a key vector (what it offers to be found by) and a value vector (what it hands over if chosen). Q and K below are random with dk dimensions, so you can see how the size of the dot products behaves. Click a row label to follow one token through every step.

dk

1 · Q × Kᵀ → scores

Every query is compared with every key: an n × n table. This is the source of attention's power (any token can reach any other in one step) and of its main cost (n² work and memory).

2 · Scale by √dk

For random vectors, a dot product has variance dk. Without scaling, larger heads give larger scores, softmax saturates to one-hot, and gradients vanish. Switch the scaling off at dk = 256 to see it.

3 · Mask

A language model must not see the future, so scores for j > i become −∞. Almost every later mechanism in this timeline is, at heart, a different mask or a cheaper way to fill this table.

4 · Softmax, then × V

Each row becomes weights that sum to 1, and the output is that weighted average of value vectors. During generation, K and V of all past tokens are kept in the KV cache, which grows with every token.

What the order reveals

Vanilla attention was not wrong; it was expensive. It sends two bills, compute that grows as n² and a KV cache that grows with every token, and it needs position supplied from outside. Every later card is somebody trying to pay less of one of them. Put in date order, the cards show which bill the field was paying at each moment.

Compute was attacked first, and mostly abandoned

The first wave (2019–20: fixed sparse patterns, top-k, sliding windows, linear attention) went after the n² compute bill. After FlashAttention (May 2022) made exact attention fast on GPUs, frontier models largely dropped approximate attention for training. Only windows survived, as one layer type among several.

Memory was named in 2019 but paid for in 2023–24

MQA identified the decode-time KV bill in November 2019. The field acted on it only once serving at scale made it the binding cost: GQA (May 2023), then MLA (May 2024). The fixes move from sharing heads, to shrinking each entry, to storing fewer entries (CSA, 2026).

Length came in two waves, and one was driven by a Reddit post

First, how to represent position (relative positions 2018, RoPE and ALiBi 2021). Then, once open LLaMA weights shipped with a 2K window, how to stretch existing models: PI, NTK-aware and YaRN appeared within about ten weeks in mid-2023. NTK-aware came from the community, not a lab. DroPE (Dec 2025) questions whether explicit position is needed at long range at all.

Old ideas return once their missing piece exists

Top-k (2019) came back as DSA (2025) once a cheap indexer could choose the k. Linear attention (2020) came back as Gated DeltaNet (2024) once the delta rule and gating fixed its memory, and it returned as most layers of a hybrid rather than as a replacement. Fixed sparse blocks (2019) came back as trained, GPU-shaped blocks in NSA and CSA.

Exactness is never fully given up

Every long-context design keeps some exact path: a window of recent tokens, a few full-attention layers, or a selected set of real tokens. The arguments are about how much exactness to keep, and where.

What the pattern suggests comes next

A reading of the trend, not a forecast. At a million tokens the far past is either stored (linear growth) or pooled (lossy). Expect effort on deciding what to keep: learned, content-dependent compression and eviction, better selectors, and memory that crosses chunk boundaries. Expect position to become less explicit at long range, and the ratio of exact to cheap layers to be tuned per workload rather than fixed.

The architecture has a workload

What would you actually pick?

No mechanism here is good or bad in isolation. The same choice can be right for a 2K-token chatbot and wrong for a million-token agent.

WorkloadReasonable choiceWhy, and what you accept
2K–8K chatbot, small model, one GPUDense causal attention, RoPE, GQA (or MQA), FlashAttention kernelsAt this length the n² term is cheap and the KV cache is small. Exact attention gives the best quality per parameter; anything fancier adds complexity for little saving.
128K assistant serving many usersGQA or MLA, RoPE extended with YaRN, some sliding-window layersThe KV cache sets how many users fit on a GPU. MLA shrinks it most but needs custom kernels; GQA is the well-supported middle. Window layers cut cost but can't retrieve far facts on their own, so keep global layers.
1M-token coding agentCompressed + sparse attention (CSA/HCA-style) or a hybrid of Gated DeltaNet layers with a few full-attention layersDense attention over 1M tokens is too costly per step. You accept lossy or selective access to the far past: the model reads compressed blocks or a learned top-k, and can miss a detail the selector didn't pick.
Endless stream (voice, live chat) with fixed memorySliding window + attention sinks, or a linear/recurrent layerMemory stays constant forever. Anything that falls out of the window is gone, so this suits "recent context matters most", not "recall what was said an hour ago".
Extend an existing 4K RoPE model to 32K–128K cheaplyYaRN with a short fine-tune; DroPE if you can do a short recalibration and want zero-shot extensionNo retraining from scratch. YaRN is proven in production; DroPE is newer and less tested at frontier scale.
New long-context model trained from scratchNatively trainable sparse attention (NSA/DSA family) or a linear-attention hybrid, plus gated attention outputYou control pretraining, so the model can learn to use sparse selection or recurrent state. The cost is engineering: custom kernels, indexers, and more hyperparameters.
Where confident sentences go wrong

Dates to watch

Each item below is a place where a quick summary or a reading list would put something in the wrong order.

Learned positions came before sinusoidal

Learned absolute position embeddings were in ConvS2S (8 May 2017). The Transformer paper (12 Jun 2017) introduced sinusoids and cites ConvS2S for the learned version, reporting nearly identical results for both.

"DeepSeek sparse attention" is three different things

NSA is a paper (16 Feb 2025). DSA shipped with DeepSeek-V3.2-Exp (29 Sep 2025). CSA and HCA arrived with DeepSeek-V4 (24 Apr 2026). Some third-party write-ups say V3.2 introduced CSA; the V4 report describes CSA as compression followed by DSA.

DroPE is not the newest item

DroPE (13 Dec 2025) predates CSA/HCA (24 Apr 2026) by four months, so it sits before DeepSeek-V4 in the timeline.

The delta rule is 2021, not 2024

Schlag, Irie & Schmidhuber added delta-rule writes to linear attention on 22 Feb 2021. The 2024 DeltaNet paper (10 Jun 2024) made it trainable in parallel; Gated DeltaNet followed on 9 Dec 2024.

NTK-aware scaling has no paper

It was a Reddit post by bloc97 on r/LocalLLaMA in late June 2023. Reddit requires sign-in, so the 29 Jun date here comes from archived copies and later citations, not the page itself.

Two "first" dates for DeepSeek-V4

The model and weights were released 24 Apr 2026 (DeepSeek API news). The arXiv version of the report is dated 26 Apr 2026, even though its ID starts with 2606. This page uses the release date.
